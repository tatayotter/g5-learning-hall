## CURRICULUM — GRADE 2 BOW

### COMPUTER
**Term 1:** What is a computer? — introducing computers as tools that help people work, learn, communicate, and play; recognizing everyday examples of computers (desktop computer, laptop, tablet, smartphone) at home, school, and in the community; Main parts of a desktop computer — monitor/screen (shows pictures and words), CPU/system unit (the "brain" that runs everything), keyboard, and mouse — identifying each part and describing its basic job in simple words; More computer parts and accessories — speakers (let us hear sound), printer (makes paper copies), webcam (takes pictures/video) — matching each part to what it lets us do; Turning a computer on and off the correct, safe way — proper steps to start up, log in if needed, and shut down properly; understanding why we should never unplug or switch off a computer without shutting it down first; Taking care of computer equipment — clean, dry hands before touching the keyboard/mouse, no food or drinks near the computer, gentle handling of cords and equipment, always asking an adult for help with plugs and wires; Introduction to the mouse — identifying its parts (left button, right button, scroll wheel) and practicing a proper, relaxed mouse grip and sitting posture
**Term 2:** Introduction to the keyboard — general layout and finding letter keys, number keys, the spacebar, and the Enter/Return key; Special keys and their jobs — Shift key (makes capital letters), Backspace/Delete key (fixes typing mistakes), Enter key (starts a new line); a light, age-appropriate introduction to using both hands comfortably on the keyboard (not full touch-typing); Typing my own name and simple familiar words on the keyboard, focusing on care and accuracy rather than speed; Opening a simple drawing/paint program and identifying its basic tools — pencil/brush, eraser, fill or paint-bucket tool, and simple shapes; Using the drawing program to color and draw simple pictures, practicing choosing colors and using the eraser tool correctly to fix mistakes; Introduction to a simple word-processing or typing program; typing short, simple sentences about myself (e.g., "I am in Grade 2.")
**Term 3:** What is the internet? — a simple, age-appropriate idea that the internet connects computers so people can find information, watch videos, and talk with others far away; Recognizing that some things online are made for children to learn and have fun, while other things are not meant for children and can feel confusing or scary — a simple, non-technical introduction to online awareness; Understanding screen-time basics — why we take breaks from screens, why we follow the time limits set by parents or teachers, and simple ways to rest our eyes; The most important online safety rule for this age group — always ask a parent, guardian, or teacher first before going online, opening an app, or visiting a website; Keeping personal information private with adult guidance — not sharing your full name, home address, school name, or photos with people you and your family don't know online; Being kind and polite when using shared or connected devices — simple digital manners such as not saying unkind things and treating others online the way we treat friends in person

### ENGLISH
**Term 1:** Phonological Awareness: recognize rhymes in chants, poems, and stories heard (e.g., cat-bat-hat, bug-rug, sun-fun word families; clapping/movement games to mark rhyme); segment onset and rime in simple words (e.g., "cat" = /c/ onset + /at/ rime; "dog" = /d/ + /og/; "sun" = /s/ + /un/).; Phonics/Word Study: identify Grade 2 level-appropriate sight words; identify alphabet letter names; decode and read words accurately and automatically by word pattern position (initial, final, medial sounds) — CVC words (e.g., mat, pen, sit, cup, dog).; Vocabulary and Word Knowledge: identify high-frequency words accurately; vocabulary about oneself and family (e.g., names for family members, feelings, daily routines) and other everyday/content-specific topics; content-specific words in Mathematics (basic symbols and terminologies, e.g., +, −, =, "sum," "number"); identify words by function — nouns (common and proper nouns; gender: boy/girl, man/woman), verbs (action/doing words), adjectives (describing words for people, places, things, animals, events, ideas, emotions), and personal pronouns (I, you, he, she, it, we, they); read words correctly for meaning and write words legibly and correctly based on word patterns.; Grammar Awareness and Grammatical Structures: identify sentences vs. non-sentences; use simple sentences to express ideas about oneself and family; use simple sentences with proper intonation (pitch, juncture, rhythm); sequence words to form meaningful telling (declarative) and asking (interrogative) sentences; identify the parts of a simple sentence (who/what is it about, what are they doing, when/where/how) in declarative and interrogative sentences; use correct capitalization and end punctuation (period, question mark) for declarative and interrogative sentences; identify discourse markers for time-order and procedural text types (e.g., first, next, then, finally).; Comprehending and Analyzing Text: read grade-level sentences with appropriate speed, accuracy, and expression; comprehend stories by noting story elements (characters, setting, events), sequencing at least three events, identifying the problem and solution, inferring a character's feelings/traits, relating story events to one's own experience, identifying cause and effect, predicting a possible ending, and giving a summary; comprehend informational texts by noting at least three significant details, identifying problem/solution, and identifying time-order and procedural text types.; Creating and Composing Text: use common expressions and polite greetings appropriate to a given situation (e.g., "Good morning," "Thank you," "Excuse me"); retell myths, legends, fables, and narrative poems in one's own words; express ideas about one's experiences (oneself/family and content topics); use basic sight, high-frequency, and content-specific words in writing/speaking; compose texts reacting to a story's character, setting, or events, or reacting to a topic; make a summary of a narrative text; express ideas using time-order and procedural text structures.
**Term 2:** Phonics/Word Study: new word patterns — CVCe words (e.g., cake, bike, home, cute) and CVVC words (e.g., rain, boat, feet, tail), on top of CVC words.; Vocabulary: vocabulary sets shift to school (e.g., classroom objects, school people, subjects, routines) and later to community (e.g., community helpers/workers, places in the community, community activities), plus continued content-specific topics and Math terms; word-function work continues (nouns incl. common/proper and gender, verbs, adjectives) and adds interrogative pronouns (who, what, which, whose) and, moving into the community stretch, possessive pronouns (mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs).; Grammar: continues declarative and interrogative sentence work (sequencing, sentence parts, capitalization/punctuation) about school and community topics, then introduces two new sentence types — imperative (commanding/requesting, e.g., "Please close the door.") and exclamatory sentences (e.g., "What a beautiful day!") — including identifying their parts and using correct capitalization and end punctuation (exclamation point) for all four sentence types by the end of the term; discourse markers expand to include description markers alongside time-order and procedural markers.; Comprehension: story and informational-text comprehension skills continue, with sequencing now covering three to four events (up from three); same skill set — story elements, problem/solution, inferring feelings/traits, relating to experience, cause/effect, predicting endings, summarizing; informational texts now noting three to four significant details and identifying time-order, procedural, and descriptive text types.; Composition: composing texts, retelling, and expressing ideas now center on school and community experiences and content topics, using the expanded vocabulary and all four sentence types as appropriate.
**Term 3:** Phonics/Word Study: word patterns expand to CVCC words (e.g., desk, lamp, sand), CCVC words with initial consonant clusters/diphthongs (e.g., stop, frog, brain, cloud), VCV words (e.g., open, evil), and VCCV words (e.g., basket, window, insect) — building on the CVC/CVCe/CVVC patterns already learned.; Vocabulary: vocabulary about the physical environment (e.g., land/water forms, weather, plants, animals, seasons) and Science-content terms, plus continued Math terms; word-function work is completed with possessive pronouns and demonstrative pronouns (this, that, these, those); introduces synonyms and antonyms of words (e.g., big/large, happy/sad).; Grammar: all four sentence types (declarative, interrogative, imperative, exclamatory) are used, sequenced, parsed into parts (subject/who-what and predicate/what-are-they-doing, when/where/how), and correctly capitalized and punctuated about physical-environment topics; discourse markers continue across time-order, procedural, and descriptive text types.; Comprehension: continues sequencing three to four events; summarizing narrative texts and identifying the main message/lesson of a story; informational text comprehension continues noting three to four details and identifying text type (time-order, procedural, descriptive).; Composition: composing and reacting to texts, retelling, and summarizing narrative texts, now centered on physical-environment and Science-content topics, using synonyms/antonyms and all sentence types as appropriate.

### SCIENCE
**Term 1:** Living vs. nonliving things — key characteristics that living things breathe, eat/take in food, grow, move on their own, respond to stimuli (light, touch, sound), and reproduce, while nonliving things do not; sorting real objects and pictures (plant, dog, rock, toy car, cloud) into living/nonliving groups; Classifying things in the immediate environment (home, school, yard) as living or nonliving; recognizing once-living materials (dried leaf, piece of wood, cotton, paper) as different from truly nonliving things like stone or metal; Basic needs of living things — air, food/nutrients, water, space, and shelter; comparing how plants, animals, and humans meet these same basic needs in different ways; External parts of the human body and their general job — head, trunk (chest/back), arms, hands, legs, feet; simple vocabulary for major features (hair, face, fingers, toes); The five sense organs — eyes (sight), ears (hearing), nose (smell), tongue (taste), skin (touch) — matching each organ to what it detects, using hands-on games (guess-the-smell, guess-the-sound); How sense organs work together during everyday tasks — e.g., smelling and tasting food, seeing and hearing traffic before crossing a street, feeling texture and temperature
**Term 2:** Common plants found in the community (trees, shrubs, vegetables, flowers); basic external parts of a plant — root, stem, leaf, flower, fruit, seed — and each part's simple job (roots hold the plant and take in water, leaves make food using sunlight); What plants need to live and grow — sunlight, water, air, soil, and space; simple guided observation of a growing seed or potted plant over several days, recording changes through drawings; Common animals found in the community, classified by where they usually live (land, water, air) and how they move (walk, swim, fly, crawl, hop); Basic needs of animals (food, water, air, shelter) and simple ways animals get food, find shelter, and protect themselves (shells, running fast, hiding, blending with surroundings); Simple life cycles — a plant's journey from seed to seedling to full-grown plant; an animal's simple life-stage example such as egg-to-chick or caterpillar-to-butterfly, told as an easy step-by-step story; Caring for plants and animals — watering and not damaging plants, feeding and being gentle with pets, not littering or harming wild animals, and why taking care of them matters
**Term 3:** Introduction to matter — everything around us (including our own bodies) is made of matter; describing objects using observable properties such as color, shape, size, texture, and whether they feel hard or soft; The three states of matter — solid, liquid, and gas — using everyday examples (ice cube, glass of water, steam from a boiling pot) and simple properties (solids keep their own shape, liquids take the shape of their container, gases spread out and fill space); Simple changes in matter observed in daily life — ice melting into water, wet clothes drying in the sun (evaporation), water boiling into steam — described in plain, everyday words without technical terms; Push and pull as the two basic ways to make things move; how a push or pull can start, stop, speed up, slow down, or change the direction of an object's motion, shown through simple play activities (pushing a toy car, pulling a rope); Simple machines that make work easier — lever (seesaw, bottle opener), wheel and axle (doorknob, toy car wheels), and inclined plane (ramp, slide) — identifying examples around the home and school; More simple machines — pulley (flagpole rope, well bucket) and wedge (knife, axe, doorstop) — with simple hands-on or picture-based exploration of how each makes a task easier

# MTAP Grade 6 Expansion Pack — Strands & Archetypes

**Status:** third grade drafted. Evidence base is broader than any other grade (4 independent real sources, including a quiz platform and a named 2019-adjacent competition-style set), but two archetypes common at lower grades (lever, mixture) are **confidently absent** here rather than just unconfirmed — a genuine grade-level difference, not a research gap.
**Shared spec:** self-paced structure rationale, sourcing policy, mastery-threshold design, and the canonical Question Template Spec all live in [mtap-expansion-overview.md](mtap-expansion-overview.md) — this doc holds only Grade 6's strand/archetype list.
**Evidence base:** 4 independent real documents (2 pdfcoffee + 1 quizalize quiz + 1 scribd, thin) — see the [overview's findings table](mtap-expansion-overview.md#cross-grade-findings--verified-against-real-question-text-tier-3) for exact quotes.

## Structure: Strand → Archetype → Tier

7 strands (independently derived for this grade), each broken into problem archetypes at 3 difficulty tiers (Easy → Average → Difficult), mastery-gated per the [threshold design in the overview](mtap-expansion-overview.md#mastery-thresholds--scaffolded-design-applies-per-archetype-per-grade). A capstone Mixed Trainer Track caps the grade.

---

### Strand 1 — Number Sense
- **GCF/LCM** — real quotes: "GCF of 91, 65, 338," "LCM of 147 and 63," "GCF of 24 and 36," "LCM of 12 and 36" — 4 independent confirmations, the most heavily-evidenced archetype in this doc.
- **Prime factorization with exponents & factorial number theory** — real quotes: "39! = 39×38×...×2×1. What is the power of 3 if 39! is written to its prime factorization?", trailing-zeroes-in-factorial simplification. Distinctly advanced, not seen at any other grade checked.
- **Repeating-decimal-to-fraction conversion** — real quote: "What is 0.151515... if written in fraction?"

### Strand 2 — Fractions, Ratio & Proportion, Percentage
- Fraction operations
- **Ratio & proportion** — real quotes: "divide a certain number of candies in the corresponding ratio of 1:2:4:6," "two numbers are in the ratio 4:5," "three numbers are in the ratio of 2:3:5" — confirmed up to 4-part ratios, a step beyond the simple a:b ratios at G4.
- **Percentage, discount, and profit/loss** — real quotes: "a price is reduced by 20%... to restore the reduced price to the original value, by how much must it be increased?" (percent-of-a-reduced-value, a classic "reverse percent" trap), "sale price was reduced by 20%, then reduced by another 10%... total reduction from original," "40% of what number is 25% of 40?" (percent-of-percent), winning-percentage-threshold framing ("must win 60% of all games — how many of the remaining 15 must it win").

### Strand 3 — Patterns & Algebra
- **Sequences** — real quotes: "what is the missing number in the sequence? 1, 4, 4, 7, 10, 16, 25, 40, ___," "what is the seventh number in the sequence 1, 2, 5, 10, 17, ...?" — confirmed non-arithmetic, alternating-rule sequences, a step up from lower grades' simple arithmetic next-term patterns.
- **Linear equations & polynomial substitution** — real quotes: "37 × (N − 4) = 268. What is N?", "if −2 is substituted for x in the expression x³ − 4x² − kx − 20..." — the polynomial-substitution item is the most advanced algebra confirmed at any grade in this pack.

### Strand 4 — Geometry & Measurement
- Circle area/circumference, cylinder volume, 3D solid properties (e.g. "how many edges are there on a triangular-based prism")
- **Percentage-change geometry** — real quote: "the length of a rectangle is increased by 15% and the width is decreased by 20%. Find the percentage change in the area" — a genuine bridge archetype combining Strand 2's percentage skill with geometry, confirmed only at this grade.
- **Similar figures / shadow-proportion problems** — real quote: "if a 3-meter post casts a shadow 7 decimeters long..." — a new proportion sub-archetype not seen at other grades.
- Supplementary/complementary angle relationships, including a sum-is-a-counting-number framing.

### Strand 5 — Statistics & Probability
- **Averages, including weighted/combined averages** — real quotes: "Elsa's grades in Math from 1st to 3rd grading are 88, 91, 92. What must be her grade in 4th grading to get an average of 92?" (reverse-solve for a missing grade given a target average — unambiguous, strongly confirmed), and a second, more advanced pattern combining separate groups' averages into one ("average of 2 numbers is 24, average of four numbers is 36, what is the average of 6 numbers" — this exact phrasing came through a summarized fetch, not raw text, so its precise setup is somewhat ambiguous; the underlying **combined/weighted-average archetype it points to is real and standard**, and is what the generation spec below is built around, rather than the ambiguous exact wording).
- **Probability** — real quotes, confirmed 3 times across 2 sources: "there are 10 black balls and 15 red balls in the urn — what is the probability?" (recurring across 2 fetches, likely one canonical item), and "he spins 100 times, how many times will it likely point to blue?" (a frequency-projection framing, distinct from a simple single-draw probability).

### Strand 6 — Classic Word-Problem Archetypes
- **Age problems** — real quotes: "Karen is twice as old as Anna. The difference between their ages is 12. How old is Karen?", "Vince is twice his sister's age who is 8 years old. Their mother's age is twice the sum of their ages" (a compound, 3-person version), "John is 3 less than twice the age of Janet."
- **Work problems** — real quotes: "Ian finished 5 boxes in 2 hours while Joey finished 10 boxes in 3 hours" (comparative-rate framing, not the classic "combined work" pipe-filling setup seen at lower grades), "a drain can empty a full bathtub in 15 minutes."
- **Relative-motion problems** — real quotes: a train-passing-a-tunnel/bridge relative-length problem, a 1000m-race relative-position problem ("Ana finished 200 meters ahead"), "Elena is jogging in a circular track... 5 laps" — a genuinely more advanced motion sub-family than lower grades' simple speed-distance-time, involving relative position/length rather than a single mover's rate.
- **Digit-rearrangement/permutation puzzles** — real quote: "the digits of the number 4765 can be rearranged to form different numbers" — a distinct archetype from lower grades' reversed-digit (`10t+u`) relationship problems; this is a counting/permutation question, not an algebraic digit-relationship one.
- **Money/pricing problems** — real quotes: "Dan has P1000. He bought a polo shirt for ₱395" and "90 pieces of bread is worth ₱135.00. How much is 60 pieces cost?" — general shopping/pricing, not the classic mixed-denomination coin-counting problems seen at G2/G3.
- **Lever/balance and mixture problems: confirmed absent, not merely unconfirmed.** Checked across all 4 real G6 sources — zero hits for either. Deliberately excluded from this strand rather than included with a caveat, unlike Grade 5's situation with the same two archetypes.

### Strand 7 — Mixed Trainer Track (capstone)
- Shuffled, timed sets drawn from all strands at a matching tier, majority-easy weighting.

## Per-archetype generation specs

Each entry: the parametric shape and how Easy → Average → Difficult scaffold onto each other. Schema/checklist: [Question Template Spec](mtap-expansion-overview.md#question-template-spec).

### Strand 1 — Number Sense

**GCF/LCM**
- Easy: direct GCF/LCM of two numbers.
- Average: GCF/LCM of three numbers (real confirmed pattern: "GCF of 91, 65, 338").
- Difficult: GCF and LCM needed together in one word problem (e.g. simplify a ratio via GCF, then solve a cycle/syncing question via LCM).

**Prime factorization & factorial number theory**
- Easy: express a number as a product of prime factors.
- Average: find the exponent of a specific prime in a number's prime factorization.
- Difficult: find the exponent of a specific prime within a factorial's prime factorization (the real confirmed "power of 3 in 39!" pattern), or count trailing zeroes in a factorial — both require recognizing that a factorial's factor count for prime p is `⌊n/p⌋ + ⌊n/p²⌋ + ⌊n/p³⌋ + ...`.

**Repeating-decimal-to-fraction conversion**
- Easy: a single-digit repeating decimal (e.g. `0.333...`) to a fraction.
- Average: a two-digit repeating block (the real confirmed `0.151515...` pattern).
- Difficult: a decimal with both a non-repeating and a repeating part (e.g. `0.1666...`).

### Strand 2 — Fractions, Ratio & Proportion, Percentage

**Ratio & proportion**
- Easy: two-part ratio, direct scaling.
- Average: three-part ratio, find one part given the total or another part (real confirmed "ratio of 2:3:5" pattern).
- Difficult: a multi-part ratio combined with a real-world constraint (real confirmed "divide candies in ratio 1:2:4:6" — total plus 4-way split).

**Percentage, discount, and profit/loss**
- Easy: direct percent-of-a-number, or a single discount.
- Average: percent-of-percent (real confirmed "40% of what number is 25% of 40?"), or a sequential double-discount (20% off, then another 10% off — NOT the same as 30% off, a genuine common-error trap).
- Difficult: reverse-percent problems — given a reduced/changed value, find the original, or find what percent increase restores a percent decrease (real confirmed "price reduced by 20%... by how much must it be increased to restore the original value?" — the answer is 25%, not 20%, because the base changed).

### Strand 3 — Patterns & Algebra

**Sequences**
- Easy: simple arithmetic sequence, next term.
- Average: alternating-rule sequence (e.g. add 3, then add 3, then add 6, then add 3... — real confirmed pattern "1,4,4,7,10,16,25,40").
- Difficult: nth-term of a non-arithmetic sequence requiring the underlying rule to be found before it can be extended (real confirmed "7th number in 1,2,5,10,17...").

**Linear equations & polynomial substitution**
- Easy: one-step linear equation.
- Average: multi-step linear equation with the unknown inside a compound expression (real confirmed `37×(N−4)=268`).
- Difficult: substitute a value into a polynomial expression containing an unknown coefficient, then solve for that coefficient (real confirmed pattern using `x³−4x²−kx−20` with x=−2 substituted, solve for k).

### Strand 4 — Geometry & Measurement

**Circle & solid geometry**
- Easy: direct circumference/area/volume formula plug-in.
- Average: reverse problem (given area/volume, find a dimension), or a solid-property fact (edges/faces/vertices of a named 3D shape).
- Difficult: a relationship between two circles/solids (real confirmed "difference between radii of two circles... difference between circumferences").

**Percentage-change geometry**
- Shape: one or more dimensions of a figure change by stated percentages; find the resulting percentage change in area/volume.
- Easy: one dimension changes, find the new area directly.
- Average: two dimensions change in the same direction (both increase or both decrease), find the percentage change in area.
- Difficult: two dimensions change in opposite directions (real confirmed "length +15%, width −20%, find % change in area") — deliberately counterintuitive, since the area doesn't simply net to −5%.

**Similar figures / shadow-proportion**
- Easy: direct proportion between two similar figures' corresponding measurements.
- Average: a real-world shadow/height proportion (real confirmed post-and-shadow pattern), solve for an unknown height or length.
- Difficult: two proportion relationships chained (e.g. object A's shadow relates to a known height, and object B's shadow relates to object A).

### Strand 5 — Statistics & Probability

**Averages, including weighted/combined**
- Easy: direct average of a small list.
- Average: reverse-solve for a missing value given a target average (real confirmed "Elsa's grades" pattern — three grades given, find the fourth needed to reach a target average).
- Difficult: combine two separately-averaged groups into one overall average — requires converting each group's average back to a sum, combining, then re-dividing by the combined count (the real confirmed pattern this archetype is built around, per the ambiguity note above).

**Probability**
- Easy: single-event probability as a simple fraction (real confirmed urn/ball pattern).
- Average: frequency-projection framing — given a probability, project how many times an event will occur over N trials (real confirmed spinner "spins 100 times" pattern).
- Difficult: two sequential events, or a complementary/"at least one" framing — not directly confirmed at G6 in the sources checked, included as a natural extension of the confirmed Easy/Average tiers rather than an independently-sourced claim (flagged honestly, same treatment as any inferred content in this pack).

### Strand 6 — Classic Word-Problem Archetypes

**Age problems**
- Easy: two-person direct relationship (real confirmed "Karen is twice as old as Anna, difference is 12").
- Average: reverse of Easy, or a "N less than twice" framing (real confirmed "John is 3 less than twice Janet's age").
- Difficult: three-person compound relationship (real confirmed "Vince/sister/mother" pattern — mother's age tied to the *sum* of the other two).

**Work problems**
- Easy: single worker/process rate (real confirmed "drain empties a bathtub in 15 minutes").
- Average: comparative rates between two workers, not combined — find who's faster or by how much (real confirmed "Ian 5 boxes/2 hours vs Joey 10 boxes/3 hours" comparison).
- Difficult: the two workers' rates are combined into a joint completion time — the classic "working together" setup, a genuine step up from the Average tier's comparison-only framing.

**Relative-motion problems**
- Easy: single mover, direct speed-distance-time.
- Average: relative position between two movers in the same race/track (real confirmed "Ana finished 200m ahead in a 1000m race").
- Difficult: relative-length problems — an object's own length must be found from how long it takes to pass two different fixed points (real confirmed train-passing-tunnel/bridge pattern) — a genuinely distinct reasoning move from Average's position-comparison.

**Digit-rearrangement/permutation puzzles**
- Easy: count how many distinct arrangements of a small set of (distinct) digits are possible.
- Average: count arrangements under a constraint (e.g. must be even, must start with a nonzero digit).
- Difficult: count arrangements with repeated digits, or find a specific-rank arrangement (e.g. "what is the 15th largest rearrangement").

**Money/pricing problems**
- Easy: direct unit-price × quantity, or a discount off a stated price.
- Average: proportional pricing — given a price for N items, find the price for M items (real confirmed "90 pieces of bread P135, how much for 60 pieces").
- Difficult: a multi-item purchase with change, combined with a percent discount.

## Worked example

Strand 5, Averages (combined/weighted), Difficult tier — chosen because it's the archetype whose exact source phrasing was ambiguous (see the note above), making it the best test that a fully mechanical spec can stand on its own without needing the original wording resolved.

```
Skeleton: "The average of {n1} numbers is {avg1}. The average of {n2} DIFFERENT
           numbers is {avg2}. What is the average of all {n1}+{n2} numbers
           combined?"

Params:   n1 ∈ [2, 5], n2 ∈ [2, 5] with n1 ≠ n2 (REQUIRED — see collision note
          below), avg1 ∈ [10, 50], avg2 ∈ [10, 50] with avg1 ≠ avg2
Constraint: sum1 = n1×avg1, sum2 = n2×avg2 — both are automatically integers
            since avg1, avg2, n1, n2 are all drawn as integers, so no
            divisibility constraint is needed here (unlike the Age Problems
            template) — the combined average itself may legitimately render
            as a fraction/decimal, which is mathematically correct and
            should NOT be "fixed" by constraining it to an integer; that
            would misrepresent how real combined-average problems work.

CAUGHT IN ACCURACY PASS: whenever n1 = n2, the true combined average
algebraically reduces to exactly (avg1+avg2)/2 — which is d1's formula.
That's not a rare per-draw coincidence, it's a deterministic identity: if
n1=n2=n, correct = (n·avg1+n·avg2)/(2n) = (avg1+avg2)/2 = d1, for ANY
avg1/avg2. Requiring n1≠n2 removes this the same way Grade 4's q≠1 fix
did — structurally, not by relying on a per-draw redraw check to catch it
every single time. (Verified the other four pairs — {correct,d2}, {correct,d3},
{d1,d2}, {d1,d3} — have no such structural collision given n1,n2≥2; {d2,d3}
wasn't algebraically ruled out but is covered by the standard per-draw check.)

Answer formula:  combined_avg = (n1×avg1 + n2×avg2) / (n1+n2)

Distractor formulas (each a named plausible slip):
  d1 = (avg1 + avg2) / 2                    // simple average of the two
                                              // averages, ignoring group sizes
                                              // entirely — the single most
                                              // common real error for this
                                              // archetype
  d2 = (n1×avg1 + n2×avg2) / 2               // divided by 2 (number of GROUPS)
                                              // instead of n1+n2 (number of
                                              // ITEMS)
  d3 = (avg1 + avg2) / (n1 + n2)             // added the averages directly
                                              // instead of converting to sums
                                              // first

Scaffold note: relies on the Average-tier skill of converting a single
               average back into a sum (average × count = sum) — Difficult
               requires doing that TWICE, for two separate groups, then
               correctly re-dividing by the combined count rather than the
               group count. d1 and d2 are both real, well-documented
               student errors for this exact archetype, not invented ones.
```

## Follow-up research pass (2026-09-05)

Triggered by a simple observation during SEC-pack content planning: this grade's archetype count (16) was lower than every other grade (19-22), which lined up suspiciously well with this grade having the thinnest sourcing (3 documents vs. 4-5 elsewhere) — a research-depth artifact, not evidence that Grade 4 content is genuinely narrower. Two more real sources were fetched and checked against three specific candidate gaps:

1. **Two-unknowns Sum & Product — CONFIRMED, added below.** This grade had *no* two-unknowns archetype at all, unlike Grade 2 (sum/difference, product) and other grades. Found once in a first new source ("sum of two numbers is 20, quotient is 3" — sum-and-*quotient*, not corroborated elsewhere), but a second new source independently confirmed a *different*, recurring sum-and-**product** version across three separate citations: "find two numbers whose sum is 21 and whose product is 98" (2004 Elimination Q11 *and* 2005 Elimination Q12 — same item recurring across years) and "sum is 24, product is 135" (2008 Division Orals A.9). Sum-and-product is the real, multi-cited archetype; sum-and-quotient is dropped as an uncorroborated single mention.
2. **Number properties (commutative/associative/distributive/identity) — CHECKED AND REJECTED.** Appeared once ("which property of multiplication is shown in 8×1=8?"), then explicitly searched for and *not found* in either of the two follow-up sources. Same shape as this pack's own Grade 3 Ratio correction: a single-source lead that doesn't survive a real cross-check. Not added.
3. **Decimal operations — mixed, left open.** Decimal place-value/rounding questions ("5.36 is equal to how many hundredths," "round 458.5826 to the nearest hundredth") appear in both follow-up sources, but both cite an identically-worded "2008 Elimination, Question 5" — a sign these two SlideShare postings may share a common upstream source rather than being fully independent, so this doesn't meet the same bar as the sum-and-product finding above. Standalone decimal *addition* (e.g. "0.8 + 4.5 + 3.6") and unit-fraction decomposition ("3/5 is the sum of 3 unit fractions") each appeared exactly once and were not specifically re-checked. **Not added to the strand list — flagged as open leads, not silently dropped** (see "What's still open" below).

## Structure: Strand → Archetype → Tier

7 strands (independently derived for this grade — not Grade 5's strand list reused), each broken into problem archetypes at 3 difficulty tiers (Easy → Average → Difficult), mastery-gated per the [threshold design in the overview](mtap-expansion-overview.md#mastery-thresholds--scaffolded-design-applies-per-archetype-per-grade). A capstone Mixed Trainer Track caps the grade once its strands are cleared.

---

### Strand 1 — Number Sense
- **Place value & digit-counting puzzles** — real quotes: "the value of 3 in 93 086," "how many numbers from 40 to 70 contain the digit 5" (a combinatorics-flavored digit puzzle distinct from Grade 5's reversed-digit `10t+u` type)
- **Rounding & estimation** — "round 23,503 to the nearest thousand," "round each addend to the nearest thousand" (round-then-operate, not just round-alone)
- **Divisibility rules & remainders** — "what is the remainder when 7,893 is divided by 26," plus the alternating-digit-sum divisibility-by-11 trick
- **GCF/LCM** — "GCF of 36, 24, 48," "LCM of 36, 27, 54," plus a real LCM-*cycle* word problem ("Maria eats every 3 hours, Jane every 4 hours — when do they next eat together")

### Strand 2 — Fractions, Decimals, Ratio & Proportion, Percentage
- Fraction/mixed-number operations and conversion ("change 8 7/12 to an improper fraction")
- **Ratio & proportion** — real quotes: "ratio of adults to kids 5∶2," "ratio of 2:3... sum of the numbers," "two numbers have a ratio of 3:5 and one number is 16 more than the other"
- **Percentage & discount** — "a pair of shoes marked ₱450 was sold at a discount of 15%," "the jeans has a discount of 12%," "winning percentage" framing (wins/total games)

### Strand 3 — Patterns & Algebra
- **Sequence / nth-term patterns** — now confirmed with a real quote from a 4th source, a named 2019 MMC Grade 4 Divisionals document: *"Find the sum of the numbers in the 20th triple: (1,4,7), (2,6,9), (3,8,11), (4,10,13), ..."* [answer: 107] — an arithmetic-sequence-of-triples pattern at the Difficult tier. (Previously flagged as inferred-from-continuity only; closed in a follow-up pass. That same source also cross-validated several other archetypes in this doc word-for-word — see [overview](mtap-expansion-overview.md) for the source note.)
- **Two-unknowns Sum & Product** — added in the 2026-09-05 follow-up pass. Real quotes: "find two numbers whose sum is 21 and whose product is 98" (2004 Elimination Q11, recurring again verbatim in 2005 Elimination Q12), "sum is 24, product is 135" (2008 Division Orals A.9) — 3 independent citations across 2 sources, the strongest-cited new addition in this pass. Distinct from Grade 2's sum/difference and product-only two-unknowns variants — this one requires finding a factor pair of the product that also matches the target sum.

### Strand 4 — Geometry & Measurement
- Perimeter (including reverse problems: "perimeter of a regular hexagon of side 12 2/3 meters")
- Area, including reverse area→side problems
- **Angles** — real quote: "two supplementary angles are in the ratio 2:3. Find the bigger angle" (angles + ratio combined — a natural bridge archetype between Strand 2 and Strand 4)

### Strand 5 — Statistics
- **Averages** — real quote: "a basketball team scores an average of 53 points per game in its first 4 games and an average of 52 points per game in its first 5 games. How many points did the team score in its 5th game?" — this exact reverse/discard-and-recompute average pattern is the confirmed G4 shape, distinct from Grade 5's simpler "average of a list" starting point.

### Strand 6 — Classic Word-Problem Archetypes
- **Age problems** — real quote: "Maria is two more than twice the age of her brother John. If John is 6 years old, how old is Maria?"
- **Lever/balance problems** — real quote (2nd G4 source): "two pieces of silver together weigh 55 grams... an additional weight of 11 grams is placed with the smaller piece" — confirms lever/balance reasoning at G4, cross-validating the Grade 3 finding of the same archetype.
- **Rate/motion problems** — real quotes: "a cyclist covered 50 kilometers at a speed of 12.5 km/hr. How long did it take?" and "the water in Angat Dam goes up 12 cm in 30 minutes — how many centimeters does the Dam go up every minute" (a rate-of-change framing, not just distance/speed/time)
- **Custom-defined-operation puzzles** — real quote: "he writes 3@2 = 3×3−2×2. Using John's technique, what is the value of 6@4?" — a distinctive G4 archetype not confirmed at any other grade checked: a symbol is defined by an arbitrary rule, then applied to a new pair of numbers.

### Strand 7 — Mixed Trainer Track (capstone)
- Shuffled, timed sets drawn from all strands at a matching tier, majority-easy weighting, matching the real elimination round's tiering feel.

## Per-archetype generation specs

Each entry: the parametric shape and how Easy → Average → Difficult scaffold onto each other. Schema/checklist these must satisfy: [Question Template Spec](mtap-expansion-overview.md#question-template-spec).

Full parametric templates below (2026-09-08) — the same skeleton/params/constraints/answer-formula/distractor treatment the Custom-defined-operation worked example demonstrates and Grade 3's pass established, done ahead of generation. Lessons carried forward from Grade 3's actual generation run: never let a distractor formula be structurally *always* equal to the answer (not just usually), never leave a uniqueness/redraw condition unverified as achievable, and force any two independently-drawn "different item/name" slots to actually be distinct.

### Strand 1 — Number Sense

**Place value & digit-counting puzzles**

- Easy:
  ```
  Skeleton: "What is the value of the digit {d} in {N}?"
  Params: N = random 5-6 digit integer. d = a digit occurring EXACTLY ONCE in
          N (avoids "which occurrence" ambiguity). Constraint: d's position
          (0-indexed from the right) >= 1 — never the ones digit, so the
          bare-digit distractor below can never equal the answer.
  Answer: d * 10^position.
  Distractors:
    e1 = d                          // forgot to multiply by place value
    e2 = d * 10^(position - 1)      // one place value too low
    e3 = d * 10^(position + 1)      // one place value too high
  ```
- Average:
  ```
  Skeleton: "How many numbers from {low} to {high} contain the digit {d}?"
            (real quote pattern: "how many numbers from 40 to 70 contain the
            digit 5").
  Params: low random multiple of 10 in [10,60]. high = low + random(20,40).
          d = random digit 0-9.
  Answer: count of n in [low,high] whose decimal string contains d.
  Distractors:
    e1 = answer ± 1                                    // a miscount by one
    e2 = count of n where the UNITS digit only equals d // ignored other positions
    e3 = (high - low + 1) - answer                       // counted the complement (did NOT contain d) instead
  Collision check: verify e1/e2/e3 differ from the answer at generation —
                   e2 can coincide with the answer for a narrow range, redraw if so.
  ```
- Difficult:
  ```
  Skeleton: "How many numbers from {low} to {high} contain the digit {d} but
            not the digit {m}?" (compound condition, per the doc's own framing).
  Params: low random multiple of 10 in [50,150]. high = low + random(40,80).
          d, m distinct random digits.
  Answer: count of n in [low,high] containing d AND not containing m.
  Distractors:
    e1 = count containing d only, ignoring the "not m" condition
    e2 = answer ± 1
    e3 = count containing m but not d (the two conditions swapped)
  ```

**Rounding & estimation**

- Easy:
  ```
  Skeleton: "Round {N} to the nearest {place}."
  Params: N random 4-6 digit integer. place from {hundred, thousand, ten
          thousand} (chosen so it's a real digit position in N).
  Answer: N rounded to that place.
  Distractors:
    e1 = N rounded to the next-coarser place (one place value too high)
    e2 = N rounded to the next-finer place (one place value too low)
    e3 = N truncated (rounded down) instead of properly rounded — only used
         when it differs from the correct answer (i.e. when N would
         actually round up)
  ```
- Average:
  ```
  Skeleton: "Round {a} and {b} to the nearest {place}, then find their sum."
            (real confirmed pattern: "round each addend to the nearest
            thousand").
  Params: a, b random 4-5 digit integers. place = thousand (or hundred for
          smaller a,b).
  Answer: round(a, place) + round(b, place).
  Distractors:
    e1 = a + b                                   // exact sum, skipped rounding entirely
    e2 = round(a + b, place)                       // rounded AFTER summing instead of before — a genuinely different method, can give a different result
    e3 = round(a, place) + b                        // only one addend rounded
  Collision check: e2 can coincide with the answer for some (a,b) — verify
                   and redraw at generation if so, matching the doc's own
                   "rounding order matters" framing for this archetype.
  ```
- Difficult:
  ```
  Skeleton: "Estimate {a} × {b} by first rounding each number to its
            greatest place value."
  Params: a random 3-4 digit, b random 2-3 digit.
  Answer: round(a, leadPlaceOfA) × round(b, leadPlaceOfB).
  Distractors:
    e1 = a × b, computed exactly (skipped the estimation step)
    e2 = round(a, leadPlaceOfA) × b                 // only one number rounded
    e3 = round(a × b, leadPlaceOfA)                  // rounded the exact product after computing, instead of rounding the factors first
  Collision check: redraw if e1/e2/e3 coincide with the answer for a given (a,b).
  ```

**Divisibility rules & remainders**

- Easy:
  ```
  Skeleton: "What is the remainder when {N} is divided by {d}?"
  Params: N random 3-4 digit integer. d random in [3,12]. Reject/redraw if
          N is exactly divisible by d.
  Answer: N mod d.
  Distractors:
    e1 = N // d                     // gave the quotient instead of the remainder
    e2 = d - (N mod d)               // distance to the next multiple, not the remainder itself
    e3 = (N mod d) ± 1               // a small arithmetic slip
  ```
- Average:
  ```
  Skeleton: "Which of these numbers is divisible by 11?" (tests the
            alternating-digit-sum rule the real citation names, via a
            4-option pick rather than a Yes/No format).
  Params: generate 4 distinct 3-4 digit candidates, exactly one actually
          divisible by 11 (constructed directly rather than searched for,
          then shuffled among 3 non-multiples).
  Answer: the one multiple of 11.
  Distractors: the 3 non-multiples.
  ```
- Difficult:
  ```
  Skeleton: "What is the smallest number greater than {X} that is divisible
            by {d}?" (combines the rule with a search, per the doc's framing).
  Params: X random 3-4 digit. d random in [6,13].
  Answer: X + d - (X mod d) if X mod d != 0 else X + d (must be STRICTLY
          greater than X even when X itself is already a multiple of d).
  Distractors:
    e1 = X + (d - X mod d) computed with the mod-zero case NOT special-cased (i.e. returns X itself when X is already a multiple — a real off-by-one-multiple error)
    e2 = answer - d               // the previous multiple, one step short
    e3 = answer + d                // one multiple too far
  ```

**GCF/LCM**

- Easy:
  ```
  Skeleton: "What is the {GCF or LCM} of {a} and {b}?"
  Params: pick a base g (the intended GCF) and coprime m,n in [2,9]; a=g*m,
          b=g*n — controls the GCF exactly rather than searching for it.
  Answer: g (if asked GCF) or g*m*n (if asked LCM).
  Distractors:
    e1 = the OTHER of GCF/LCM computed instead (concept swap)
    e2 = a * b                       // product mistaken for LCM (or GCF)
    e3 = a + b                        // a plain wrong operation
  ```
- Average:
  ```
  Skeleton: "{name1} eats every {a} hours and {name2} eats every {b} hours.
            If they eat together now, in how many hours will they eat
            together again?" (real confirmed LCM-cycle pattern).
  Params: a, b distinct random integers in [2,8].
  Answer: lcm(a,b).
  Distractors:
    e1 = a * b        // the product, a very common LCM guess
    e2 = a + b
    e3 = max(a,b)      // naive "just the larger cycle" guess
  ```
- Difficult:
  ```
  Skeleton: "What is the LCM of {a}, {b}, and {c}?"
  Params: three small integers in [2,12], not all sharing a trivial common structure.
  Answer: lcm(a,b,c).
  Distractors:
    e1 = lcm(a,b)          // forgot the third number
    e2 = a * b * c           // product of all three
    e3 = gcd(a,b,c)           // computed GCF instead of LCM (concept swap)
  Collision check: redraw if e1/e2/e3 coincide with the true LCM for a given triple.
  ```

### Strand 2 — Fractions, Decimals, Ratio & Proportion, Percentage

**Fraction/mixed-number operations**

- Easy:
  ```
  Skeleton: "Change {w} {n}/{d} to an improper fraction." (real quote:
            "change 8 7/12 to an improper fraction").
  Params: w random [1,12]. d random denominator in [3,12]. n random [1,d-1].
  Answer: (w*d + n)/d, as "X/d".
  Distractors:
    e1 = "w+n/d"'s numerator only, i.e. w*d expressed alone (forgot to add n)
    e2 = (w+n)/d                     // added w and n directly instead of w*d+n
    e3 = (w*d - n)/d                  // subtracted instead of added
  ```
- Average:
  ```
  Skeleton: "What is {w1} {n1}/{d1} + {n2}/{d2}?" (mixed number + fraction, unlike denominators).
  Params: w1 random [1,10]. d1 ≠ d2, both from {3,4,5,6,8,10}. n1 in [1,d1-1], n2 in [1,d2-1].
  Answer: convert w1's mixed number to improper over d1, common-denominator
          add with n2/d2, simplify back to a mixed number, reduced.
  Distractors:
    e1 = added straight across (numerators and denominators separately)
    e2 = used the product d1×d2 as the common denominator instead of the LCM, left unreduced
    e3 = added only the whole-number part to the fraction sum incorrectly (dropped w1 entirely, then re-added it post-hoc in the wrong place — modeled as w1 + n1/d1 + n2/d2 computed then w1 dropped from the final answer)
  ```
- Difficult:
  ```
  Skeleton: "{n1}/{d} + ___ = {n2}/{d}. What is the missing fraction?"
            (reverse problem, same denominator so the missing-piece skill is
            isolated).
  Params: d random denominator. n1 random [1,d-2]. n2 random [n1+1,d-1].
          Constraint: gcd(n2-n1, d) > 1 so the answer genuinely needs reducing.
  Answer: (n2-n1)/d, reduced.
  Distractors:
    e1 = n2/d                 // repeated the target instead of solving
    e2 = n1/d                  // repeated the given instead of solving
    e3 = (n2-n1)/d, unreduced   // guaranteed distinct by the gcd constraint
  ```

**Ratio & proportion**

- Easy:
  ```
  Skeleton: "The ratio of {itemA} to {itemB} is {a}:{b}. If there are {x}
            {itemA}, how many {itemB} are there?"
  Params: a, b coprime small ints [2,9]. k random [2,10]. x = a*k.
  Answer: b*k.
  Distractors:
    e1 = a*k               // repeated the given quantity
    e2 = x*b - x*a           // a plausible-looking but wrong combination
    e3 = x + (b-a)            // additive instead of multiplicative scaling
  ```
- Average:
  ```
  Skeleton: "The ratio of adults to children at an event is {a}:{b}. If
            there were {total} people in total, how many were adults?"
            (real confirmed pattern).
  Params: a, b coprime small ints. k random [3,15]. total = (a+b)*k.
  Answer: a*k.
  Distractors:
    e1 = b*k             // gave the children count instead of adults
    e2 = total // 2        // assumed an even split
    e3 = total             // repeated the given total itself
  ```
- Difficult:
  ```
  Skeleton: "Two numbers are in the ratio {a}:{b}. The larger number is
            {diff} more than the smaller. What is the larger number?"
            (real confirmed "ratio 3:5, one number 16 more" pattern).
  Params: a < b, coprime small ints. k random [2,10]. diff = (b-a)*k.
  Answer: b*k.
  Distractors:
    e1 = a*k               // the smaller number instead of the larger
    e2 = diff                // repeated the given difference itself
    e3 = (a+b)*k              // the sum of both numbers instead of the larger one
  ```

**Percentage & discount**

- Easy:
  ```
  Skeleton: "What is {p}% of {N}?"
  Params: p from {10,15,20,25,50,75}. N chosen as a multiple making the
          answer a clean integer.
  Answer: N*p/100.
  Distractors:
    e1 = N*(100-p)/100      // the complement percentage instead
    e2 = N/p                 // flipped the operation
    e3 = N*p                  // forgot to divide by 100
  ```
- Average:
  ```
  Skeleton: "A {item} marked ₱{price} was sold at a discount of {p}%. What
            was the sale price?"
  Params: price a multiple of 20 in [100,2000]. p from {10,15,20,25,30}.
  Answer: price*(1 - p/100).
  Distractors:
    e1 = price*p/100          // gave the discount AMOUNT, not the sale price
    e2 = price*(1 + p/100)      // added instead of subtracted
    e3 = price - p               // subtracted the percent number directly as if it were pesos
  ```
- Difficult:
  ```
  Skeleton: "A team has won {w} of its first {p} games in a {total}-game
            season. How many of the remaining games must they win to reach
            a season winning percentage of at least {targetPct}%?"
  Params: total a multiple of 20 in [20,60]. targetPct from {60,70,75,80}
          chosen so targetPct/100*total is a clean integer. p random <
          total, w random <= p, chosen so the needed count is positive and
          ≤ (total-p).
  Answer: max(0, ceil(targetPct/100*total) - w).
  Distractors:
    e1 = ceil(targetPct/100*total)      // forgot to subtract the games already won
    e2 = total - p                        // gave the number of remaining games itself, not the number that must be won
    e3 = w                                 // repeated the current win count
  ```

### Strand 3 — Patterns & Algebra

**Sequence / nth-term patterns**

- Easy:
  ```
  Skeleton: "What is the next term in the sequence: {t1}, {t2}, {t3}, {t4}, ___?"
  Params: a1 in [1,20]. d in [2,9]. Four given terms a1, a1+d, a1+2d, a1+3d.
  Answer: a1 + 4d.
  Distractors: e1 = a1+3d (repeated last term); e2 = a1+5d (added d twice);
    e3 = a1 + 4*(d±1) (wrong common difference).
  ```
- Average:
  ```
  Skeleton: "The first term of a sequence is {a1}, and each term after that
            increases by {d}. What is the {n}th term?"
  Params: a1 in [1,20]. d in [2,9]. n in [10,30].
  Answer: a1 + (n-1)*d.
  Distractors: e1 = a1+n*d (off-by-one term count); e2 = a1*n (wrong
    formula entirely); e3 = a1+(n-1)*(d±1) (misread common difference).
  ```
- Difficult:
  ```
  Skeleton: "Consider these groups of three numbers: ({p1},{q1},{r1}),
            ({p2},{q2},{r2}), ({p3},{q3},{r3}), ({p4},{q4},{r4}), ...
            Find the sum of the numbers in the {n}th group." (real
            confirmed pattern, exact source structure: three independent
            arithmetic sub-sequences interleaved into triples, whose SUMS
            themselves form a second arithmetic sequence).
  Params: three independent arithmetic sequences (a1,d1), (a2,d2), (a3,d3),
          each ai in [1,10], di in [1,5]. Four groups shown (i=1..4):
          pi=a1+(i-1)*d1, qi=a2+(i-1)*d2, ri=a3+(i-1)*d3. n in [15,30].
  Answer: (a1+a2+a3) + (n-1)*(d1+d2+d3).
  Distractors:
    e1 = (a1+a2+a3) + n*(d1+d2+d3)                          // off-by-one term-count on the OUTER (sum) sequence
    e2 = (a1+(n-1)*d1) + a2 + a3                              // only extended ONE of the three inner sequences to the nth group, left the other two at their group-1 values — the exact "only noticed one layer" error this archetype targets
    e3 = (a1+a2+a3) * n                                        // multiplied instead of using the arithmetic formula
  Collision check: verify against the real source example
                   ((1,4,7),(2,6,9),(3,8,11),(4,10,13), 20th
                   group=107) as a fixed regression case at generation time.
  ```

**Two-unknowns Sum & Product**

- Easy:
  ```
  Skeleton: "Two numbers have a sum of {s} and a product of {p}. What is
            the larger number?" (real confirmed "sum 21, product 98" pattern).
  Params: pick x < y directly (x in [2,10], y in [x+1,20]). s=x+y, p=x*y.
  Answer: y.
  Distractors:
    e1 = x                    // gave the smaller number
    e2 = s // 2                 // assumed an even split
    e3 = p // s                  // a plausible-looking but wrong formula
  ```
- Average:
  ```
  Skeleton: same shape as Easy, wider ranges (real confirmed "sum 24,
            product 135" pattern — more factor pairs to sift through).
  Params: x in [3,20], y in [x+1,40].
  Answer / distractors: same formulas as Easy.
  ```
- Difficult:
  ```
  Skeleton: "The sum of two numbers is {s}. Their product is {p}. Find the
            positive difference between the two numbers." (reverse-framed —
            the child must translate sum/product into a difference, not
            just identify one of the two numbers directly).
  Params: same x<y setup as Average.
  Answer: y - x.
  Distractors:
    e1 = s              // the sum itself, repeated
    e2 = p                // the product itself, repeated
    e3 = y                 // the larger number alone, not the difference
  ```

### Strand 4 — Geometry & Measurement

**Perimeter & area (incl. reverse problems)**

- Easy:
  ```
  Skeleton: "What is the perimeter of a regular hexagon with a side length
            of {side} meters?" (integer side at this tier — the real
            quote's fractional-side flavor is pushed to Difficult, where
            fraction arithmetic is a genuine step up, not just a longer number).
  Params: side random integer [3,20].
  Answer: 6*side.
  Distractors: e1 = 5*side (wrong side count); e2 = 4*side (square
    assumption); e3 = side // 6 (flipped the operation).
  ```
- Average:
  ```
  Skeleton: "A square has a perimeter of {P} meters. What is the length of one side?"
  Params: side random integer [3,50]. P = 4*side.
  Answer: side.
  Distractors: e1 = P // 2; e2 = P * 4; e3 = P - 4.
  ```
- Difficult:
  ```
  Skeleton: "What is the perimeter of a regular hexagon with a side length
            of {w} {n}/{d} meters?" (real quote pattern: side `12 2/3 m`).
  Params: w random integer [3,20]. d from {2,3,4}. n in [1,d-1].
  Answer: 6w + reduce(6n, d) expressed as a mixed number (6*(w + n/d)).
  Distractors:
    e1 = 5*(w + n/d)            // wrong side count
    e2 = 6*w + n/d                // forgot to multiply the fractional part by 6
    e3 = 6*w                       // dropped the fractional part of the side entirely
  ```

**Angles**

- Easy:
  ```
  Skeleton: "Two angles are complementary. One angle measures {a} degrees. What is the other angle?"
  Params: a random integer [10,80].
  Answer: 90 - a.
  Distractors: e1 = 180 - a (supplementary confusion); e2 = a (repeated
    the given); e3 = 90 + a.
  ```
- Average:
  ```
  Skeleton: "Two supplementary angles are in the ratio {a}:{b}. Find the
            bigger angle." (real confirmed pattern, built on the Strand 2 ratio skill).
  Params: a < b, coprime small ints.
  Answer: 180 * b // (a+b) (chosen so this divides evenly).
  Distractors:
    e1 = 180*a // (a+b)          // the smaller angle instead
    e2 = 90*b // (a+b)             // used 90 instead of 180 — complementary confusion
    e3 = 180 // (a+b)               // forgot to multiply by b
  ```
- Difficult:
  ```
  Skeleton: "Three angles on a straight line include one angle of {a}°, and
            the other two are in the ratio {p}:{q}. Find the larger of the
            two ratio angles."
  Params: a random [20,100]. p<q coprime small ints. Constraint: (180-a)
          divisible by (p+q).
  Answer: (180-a) * q // (p+q).
  Distractors:
    e1 = (180-a)*p // (p+q)        // the smaller of the two ratio angles
    e2 = 180*q // (p+q)              // forgot to subtract the known angle a first
    e3 = (180-a) // 2                 // assumed an even split instead of the ratio
  ```

### Strand 5 — Statistics

**Averages**

- Easy: same shape as Grade 3's Easy Averages (direct average of a small
  list) — kept identical since this is genuinely the same foundational
  skill; the G4-distinguishing step-up is entirely in the Average tier below.
- Average:
  ```
  Skeleton: "A team scored an average of {avg1} points per game in its
            first {n1} games, and an average of {avg2} points per game in
            its first {n2} games. How many points did the team score in
            game {n2}?" (real confirmed G4 pattern — deliberately harder
            than Grade 5's simple list-average Easy tier).
  Params: n1 random [3,6]. n2 = n1+1. avg1, avg2 chosen so
          n2*avg2 - n1*avg1 is a clean, positive, plausible single-game score.
  Answer: n2*avg2 - n1*avg1.
  Distractors:
    e1 = avg2 - avg1              // naive difference of the two averages
    e2 = avg2                       // just repeated the newer average
    e3 = n1*avg1                     // gave the FIRST total instead of the new game's score
  ```
- Difficult:
  ```
  Skeleton: "A team's average after {n1} games was {avg1}. Its average
            after {n3} games was {avg3} (n3 > n1+1). How many total points
            were scored in games {n1+1} through {n3}?" (extends the Average
            tier's two-snapshot idea to a wider gap).
  Params: n1 random [2,4]. n3 = n1 + random[2,4]. avg1, avg3 chosen so
          n3*avg3 - n1*avg1 is clean and positive.
  Answer: n3*avg3 - n1*avg1.
  Distractors:
    e1 = avg3 - avg1
    e2 = avg3 * (n3 - n1)
    e3 = n3*avg3                    // forgot to subtract the first snapshot's total
  ```

### Strand 6 — Classic Word-Problem Archetypes

**Age problems**

- Easy:
  ```
  Skeleton: "{name1} is {k} more than {mult} times the age of {name2}. If
            {name2} is {b} years old, how old is {name1}?" (real quote
            pattern: "Maria is two more than twice John's age").
  Params: mult from {2,3}. k random [1,10]. b random [3,15].
  Answer: mult*b + k.
  Distractors: e1 = mult*b (forgot +k); e2 = b+k (forgot to multiply);
    e3 = mult*(b+k) (applied k before multiplying instead of after).
  ```
- Average:
  ```
  Skeleton: same relationship, reversed — "{name1} is {k} more than
            {mult} times the age of {name2}. If {name1} is {a} years old,
            how old is {name2}?"
  Params: pick b first (as in Easy), derive a = mult*b+k, present a and solve for b.
  Answer: (a-k) / mult.
  Distractors: e1 = a // mult (forgot to subtract k first); e2 = a - k
    (forgot to divide); e3 = (a+k) // mult (sign error on k).
  ```
- Difficult:
  ```
  Skeleton: "In {y} years, {name1} will be {k} more than {mult} times as
            old as {name2} will be then. {name2} is currently {b} years
            old. How old is {name1} now?" (relationship holds at a future
            time point — two time references to track at once).
  Params: b random [3,15]. y random [2,10]. mult from {2,3}. k random [1,10].
  Answer: mult*(b+y) + k - y.
  Distractors:
    e1 = mult*(b+y) + k          // forgot to subtract y back down to "now"
    e2 = mult*b + k                // used name2's CURRENT age instead of their future age
    e3 = mult*(b+y) + k + y         // sign error, added y again instead of subtracting
  ```

**Lever/balance problems**

- Easy:
  ```
  Skeleton: "Two pieces of silver together weigh {total} grams. One piece
            weighs {diff} grams more than the other. What is the weight of
            the smaller piece?" (real confirmed pattern — a sum/difference
            algebra problem in a lever costume, per the doc's own note).
  Params: total, diff same parity (both even or both odd) so the split
          divides evenly. smaller = (total-diff)/2, must be positive.
  Answer: smaller.
  Distractors:
    e1 = (total+diff) // 2       // the larger piece, not the one asked
    e2 = total - diff              // forgot the final /2 step
    e3 = diff                       // repeated the given difference itself
  ```
- Average:
  ```
  Skeleton: "A lever is balanced with a {w1}-kg weight {d1} meters from the
            fulcrum. How heavy must a second weight be, placed {d2} meters
            from the fulcrum on the other side, to balance it?"
  Params: w1 random [2,20]. d1, d2 chosen so w1*d1 is divisible by d2.
  Answer: w1*d1 // d2.
  Distractors:
    e1 = w1*d2 // d1          // flipped which distance divides which
    e2 = w1 + d1 - d2           // a plausible-looking wrong combination
    e3 = w1*(d1+d2)              // wrong operation entirely
  ```
- Difficult:
  ```
  Skeleton: "A {w1}-kg weight is placed {d1} meters from the fulcrum on one
            side. On the other side, a {w2}-kg weight is placed {d2} meters
            from the fulcrum, along with a third weight {d3} meters from
            the fulcrum on that same side. If everything balances and the
            third weight is {w3} kg, what is {d3}?" (three points on the lever).
  Params: pick d3 first (small int), then choose w1,d1,w2,d2,w3 so that
          w1*d1 = w2*d2 + w3*d3 exactly (solve forward, not backward).
  Answer: d3.
  Distractors:
    e1 = (w1*d1 + w2*d2) // w3       // sign error, added instead of subtracted
    e2 = (w1*d1) // (w2+w3)            // wrong combination of the weights
    e3 = d3 ± 1                          // an off-by-one arithmetic slip
  ```

**Rate/motion problems**

- Easy:
  ```
  Skeleton: "A cyclist covered {dist} kilometers at a speed of {speed}
            km/hr. How long did it take?" (real confirmed pattern).
  Params: speed random [5,25]. time random [1,6]. dist = speed*time.
  Answer: time (dist/speed).
  Distractors: e1 = dist*speed (multiplied instead of divided); e2 =
    speed/dist (flipped); e3 = dist-speed (a nonsense subtraction, but a real slip).
  ```
- Average:
  ```
  Skeleton: "The water level in a dam rises {rise} cm in {minutes}
            minutes. How many centimeters does it rise per minute?" (real
            confirmed dam pattern).
  Params: minutes random [5,30]. perMinute random [1,5]. rise = perMinute*minutes.
  Answer: perMinute (rise/minutes).
  Distractors: e1 = minutes/rise (flipped); e2 = rise*minutes; e3 = rise-minutes.
  ```
- Difficult:
  ```
  Skeleton: "The water level in a dam rises {rise} cm every {minutes}
            minutes. At this rate, how many centimeters will it rise in
            {hours} hours?" (rate-of-change + a projected future value).
  Params: minutes, rise chosen for a clean per-minute rate (as in Average).
          hours random [1,5].
  Answer: (rise/minutes) * hours * 60.
  Distractors:
    e1 = (rise/minutes) * hours       // forgot to convert hours to minutes
    e2 = rise * hours                   // ignored the rate calculation, just scaled the raw rise value
    e3 = (rise/minutes) * hours * 24     // used a day's minutes instead of an hour's
  ```

**Custom-defined-operation puzzles** (Average tier is already fully worked
in the [Worked example](#worked-example) section below — Easy and
Difficult here complete the set)

- Easy:
  ```
  Skeleton: "For any two numbers, {a}@{b} = {p}×{a} + {q}×{b}. What is {x}@{y}?"
  Params: p, q small ints [2,5]. x, y random [1,15].
  Answer: p*x + q*y.
  Distractors:
    e1 = p*y + q*x           // swapped which operand gets which coefficient
    e2 = (p+q)*(x+y)           // wrong combination entirely
    e3 = p*x - q*y               // sign error
  ```
- Difficult:
  ```
  Skeleton: "For any two numbers, {a}@{b} = {p}×{a} − {q}×{b}. What is
            ({x}@{y})@{z}?" (the operation applied twice, chained).
  Params: p, q small ints, q ≠ 1 (same collision-avoidance reasoning as the
          Average-tier worked example — q=1 risks a distractor coinciding
          with the answer). x, y, z random [1,12].
  Answer: inner = p*x - q*y; final = p*inner - q*z.
  Distractors:
    e1 = p*x - q*y - q*z         // forgot to re-multiply the inner result by p before combining with z
    e2 = p*(p*x - q*y) - q*(p*z) // applied p to z as well, a plausible over-generalization of the rule
    e3 = inner                     // stopped after the first application, forgot the second @z entirely
  ```

## Worked example

Strand 6, Custom-defined-operation puzzle, Average tier — chosen because it's the most distinctive Grade 4 archetype found, not seen at any other grade checked, and a good test that the [Question Template Spec](mtap-expansion-overview.md#question-template-spec) generalizes past "standard" word-problem shapes.

```
Skeleton: "For any two numbers, {a}@{b} = {p}×{a} − {q}×{b}. Given that
           {x}@{k} = {result}, what is the value of {k}?"

Params:   p ∈ [2, 4], q ∈ [2, 3] (p ≠ q, so the operation isn't symmetric-looking —
          NOTE: q must exclude 1, see the d3 collision below),
          x ∈ [4, 12], k solved for, not drawn directly.
Constraint: pick k ∈ [2, 10] first (a clean small integer), compute
            result = p×x − q×k, THEN present the question with x and result
            given and k as the unknown — this ordering (solve forward, ask
            backward) is what makes it "Average" rather than "Easy," and
            guarantees k always comes out to the clean integer it started as.

Answer formula:  k = (p×x − result) / q

Distractor formulas (each a named plausible slip):
  d1 = (p×x + result) / q          // added instead of subtracting result
  d2 = (result − p×x) / q          // sign flipped on the whole numerator
  d3 = (p×x − result) × q          // multiplied by q instead of dividing

CAUGHT IN ACCURACY PASS: since (p×x − result) = k×q by the answer formula
rearranged, d3 = (k×q)×q = k×q². If q=1, d3 = k — a silent collision with
the correct answer, exactly the failure mode checklist item 11 exists to
catch. This is why q's range above is [2,3], not [1,3] as an earlier draft
had it — excluding q=1 makes the collision structurally impossible rather
than relying on a per-draw redraw-on-collision check to catch it every time.

Scaffold note: relies on the Easy-tier skill of direct forward substitution
               into a defined operation — Average adds the "run the defined
               operation backward, solve for the missing operand" step,
               which is exactly the reverse-equation skill Strand 2's ratio
               Average tier and Strand 6's Age Average tier also build,
               just applied to a novel (invented, not standard-arithmetic)
               operation.
```
